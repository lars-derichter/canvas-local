# AI Policy Frameworks

The reference payload `/ai-policy-build` reads in Phase A: the two published
frameworks it offers as starting points, how they combine, what the custom route
asks instead, and the English wording examples the skill translates into the
course language at runtime. Every framework claim on a policy page comes from
this file. The page itself names a framework in one line and links its resource
site; the reference list at the end is for the teacher, never for the students.

## The AI Assessment Scale (AIAS)

A five-level scale that states, per assessment task, how much AI use the task is
designed for. Perkins, Furze, Roe and MacVaugh (2024) published the first
version; Perkins, Roe and Furze (2025) revised it, and the current version is
AIAS 2.1, in which each level is a single statement of how the task is designed
and what is expected of the student. The level is chosen per task, not per
course: the implementation guide asks the teacher to pick, for each assessment,
the level that fits its learning outcomes, and a course can run tasks at
different levels side by side. Choosing a level is the start of the work, not
the end of it; the task has to be built so that the level holds.

| Level | Name             | Meaning                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ----- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | No AI            | The task is done in a controlled setting that excludes AI. Knowledge, understanding and skills are shown and assessed independently.                                                                                                        |
| 2     | AI Planning      | The task is about planning: exploring the topic, outlining, first research. AI may support that, and the quality of the planning and the ideas is assessed whether or not AI was used.                                                      |
| 3     | AI Collaboration | AI may help complete the task: ideas, drafting, feedback, refinement. The task is designed so that AI alone does not reach the required standard, and assessment covers both the work and how the AI's output was judged, changed and used. |
| 4     | Full AI          | AI involvement is expected. The goal is out of reach for AI alone or for a person alone in the time available, so assessment looks at the critical thinking and subject knowledge shown in directing the AI.                                |
| 5     | AI Exploration   | The task is built for creative AI use: solving problems, finding new insights or developing solutions in the discipline. Students and teacher may co-design the approach.                                                                   |

Level 1 is the only level that needs a controlled setting. Levels 2 to 5 all
allow AI and differ in what the task is designed to assess and in how far AI may
go, so homework and a project can sit on different levels in the same course.

**Suggest when** the course has several assessment moments that differ in how
open they are, or when the teacher wants a vocabulary students meet in other
courses too.

## The Two-Lane Approach

Liu and Bridgeman (2023) proposed the two lanes at the University of Sydney,
starting from the observation that no assessment design out-runs generative AI,
so the question becomes where the programme assures its outcomes and where it
lets students work with AI. Bridgeman and Liu (2024) answer the recurring
questions, and Bridgeman, Liu and Weeks (2024) move the design to programme
level.

- **Lane 1, secured: assessment of learning.** Supervised, in person: an
  in-class task done on the spot, an interactive oral, a viva, a supervised exam
  used sparingly. This lane assures that the programme's outcomes were met. AI
  is used only where the examiner says so.
- **Lane 2, open: assessment for and as learning.** Unsupervised. AI use is
  expected and scaffolded, the way the discipline itself works with it, and the
  task is designed so that the student learns by doing it. Liu and Bridgeman
  (2023) argue that this lane deserves the larger share of the weighting.

At programme level, not every course needs a Lane 1 moment: the secured points
are placed across the programme where its outcomes are assured, and the rest of
the assessment stays open (Bridgeman et al., 2024). For one course this means
asking whether it holds one of those secured points, and which moment that is.

**Critique.** Curtis (2025) reads the two lanes as all-or-none, AI forbidden in
one and unrestricted in the other, and argues that a middle lane is needed where
AI is allowed with limits. Corbin, Dawson and Liu (2025) argue from the other
side that telling students what is permitted is a discursive change, cheap and
not enough on its own, and that the task itself has to change so that its
validity no longer depends on students following the instructions. Read either
way, the practical answer for a course is the same: the open moments come with
rules, written down, which is what AIAS levels 2 to 4 or a custom policy do
inside Lane 2, and the secured moment is the structure. The sector-level frame
the Sydney posts sit in is the Australian regulator's discussion paper on
assessment reform in the age of AI (Lodge et al., 2023).

**Suggest when** the course has one supervised moment plus open work and the
teacher wants two words rather than five levels, or when the programme already
uses the lanes.

## Combining Them

Lane 1 is AIAS level 1: the controlled setting that excludes AI. AIAS levels 2
to 5 all live inside Lane 2: they say how far AI may go in a task that is open
anyway. The two frameworks answer different questions, where the programme
assures learning against how each task is designed, so a course can use both:
the lane tells students whether the moment is supervised, the level tells them
what AI may do there. This mapping is this skill's reading of the two side by
side, not a published one.

A worked example, a course with three assessment moments:

| Moment          | Lane   | AIAS level | What it means for the student                                                                       |
| --------------- | ------ | ---------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Homework        | Lane 2 | 3          | AI may help you understand and get unstuck; the work you hand in is yours, and you say what AI did. |
| Project         | Lane 2 | 3 or 4     | Level 3 when the project is doable without AI; level 4 when working with AI is part of the task.    |
| Supervised test | Lane 1 | 1          | No AI, in any form. The test measures what you can do on your own.                                  |

## Custom Policy

For when neither framework fits or both are overkill: a course with one
assessment moment, a small course, an institution policy that already fixes the
rules and only needs a course-specific line, or a stance neither framework
expresses (one grey zone allowed and another forbidden, a rule that turns on the
reason rather than on the task). Nothing on the page names a framework, and the
interview goes deeper because there is no published vocabulary to fall back on:
every rule has to be the teacher's own, and stated so that a student can act on
it.

Themes for the follow-up rounds, after the shared core of step 3:

- **The reason.** What students should learn by doing it themselves, and why
  that matters in this course. This becomes the page's intro.
- **The grey zones**, decided one by one: brainstorming, having something
  explained, translation, code completion, proofreading, generating examples,
  summarising sources. Each ends up allowed, allowed with a condition, or not
  allowed.
- **Breaking the rule.** What happens, and whether the page says so.
- **Per moment.** How the rule differs between homework, projects and tests.
- **The student's question.** What the teacher would answer a student who asks
  "may I use it for X". The answer usually reveals the rule behind the rules.
- **The tone.** Trust-based (the disclosure line is a conversation starter) or
  enforcement (checks, declarations, consequences).

Then the playback: every rule as one short student-facing sentence, corrected by
the teacher one by one, before the outline is proposed.

## Wording Examples

English models; the skill writes the course language. One sentence per AIAS
level, as a student reads it on the policy page:

- **Level 1, No AI:** "You do this without AI, in a supervised setting. It
  measures what you can do on your own."
- **Level 2, AI Planning:** "You may use AI to explore the topic, outline and do
  first research. The work itself is yours, and we assess the quality of your
  planning either way."
- **Level 3, AI Collaboration:** "You may use AI for ideas, drafts and feedback.
  What you hand in shows your own judgement of what the AI gave you, and we
  assess how you used it as much as the result."
- **Level 4, Full AI:** "AI is part of this task. We assess how well you direct
  it: the questions you ask, what you keep, what you reject and why."
- **Level 5, AI Exploration:** "This task is about finding out what AI can do
  for this kind of problem. Agree your approach with me before you start."

Per lane:

- **Lane 1:** "This is a secured moment: supervised, in person, and AI-free
  unless the examiner says otherwise. It is where we check what you have
  learned."
- **Lane 2:** "This is open work: you may use AI the way a professional in this
  field would, and the task is built so that you learn by doing it. Say what AI
  did."

Disclosure lines, one per submission:

- "AI used for: explaining the error message in exercise 2."
- "AI used for: nothing."
- "AI used for: a first draft of the introduction, rewritten by me."
